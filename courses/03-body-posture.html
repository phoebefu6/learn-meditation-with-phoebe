Body & posture: the body scan and how to sit

Part 0

Why this session exists

Two quiet dropout machines run in every beginner's first month. Machine one: the mental image of a cross-legged monk, which makes ordinary people with ordinary hips conclude their body is wrong for this. It is not - a chair is a fully legitimate meditation seat, and always has been. Machine two: starting with the breath, which is subtle and easy to lose. MBSR - the 8-week clinical program most of the research studies - does not start there. It assigns the body scan first, because bodily sensation is a bigger, easier target for a beginner's attention. Today you get both fixes.

Part 1 · covers posture options and discomfort

How to sit (anywhere) 6 min live

Every posture instruction ever written compresses to two rules: the spine supports itself (so alertness has a scaffold), and everything else is comfortable (so pain does not eat your attention budget). Any position that passes both rules is a meditation posture. Four pass reliably.

LiveThe four options, honestly ranked3 min▶
OptionSetupHonest note
ChairFeet flat on the floor, hands on thighs. Sit slightly away from the backrest if that is comfortable, so the spine holds itself - lean back if it is not.The default for this course. MBSR itself teaches chair sitting. Zero shame, zero asterisk.
KneelingShins on the floor, seat on a meditation bench or a firm cushion between the heels.The spine stacks almost automatically. Kind to hips, harder on ankles - pad them.
Cross-leggedOn a cushion tall enough that the knees sit level with or below the hips.Only works with open hips. Knees floating in the air means the cushion is too low or the posture is wrong for your body today. No lotus required, ever.
Lying downOn your back, knees bent or legs flat, arms at your sides.Fully valid - it is the standard body-scan position. Honest note: it is also the sleepiest, so keep it for body scans and use a seated option for breath practice when you can.
Real world

The chair confession. Ask around any long-running meditation group and you will find committed practitioners of a decade or more who have never once sat on the floor. Their attention got trained anyway. The floor is a preference, not a prerequisite.

LiveDiscomfort: the fidget-vs-pain rule3 min▶

Some discomfort will visit every sit. The skill is sorting it into two bins with two opposite responses:

  • Fidget-itch - the restless urge to shift, scratch, or check the timer; a mild ache of stillness. Sit through it. It usually passes within a minute, and noticing "urge to move" without obeying it is itself a rep - the same notice-and-return move you do with thoughts.
  • Pain - sharp, burning, numb-and-tingling, or joint pain that grows. Adjust immediately, mid-sit, no ceremony. Move the leg, change position, switch to the chair. There is no medal for enduring pain, and pushing through it is how people injure knees and quit forever.

The test when unsure: fidget-itch fades when you observe it; pain gets louder. Give it thirty seconds of honest attention, then believe the answer.

No heroism, ever Adjusting your posture is not "losing". A two-second mindful adjustment - feel the intention, move, resettle - keeps the practice going. Gritting your teeth for ten minutes trains exactly one thing: dreading the next sit.
Part 2 · covers the body scan

The body scan 7 min live

The body scan is attention moved systematically through the body - feet to head, or head to feet - resting on each zone in turn and noticing whatever sensation is there: warmth, pressure, tingling, contact with the chair, or nothing at all. You are not relaxing the zones, fixing them, or judging them. You are just visiting. Noticing without fixing is the whole instruction.

LiveWhy MBSR assigns this first, before any sitting3 min▶

In the standard MBSR curriculum, the body scan is the first formal practice assigned - weeks 1 and 2, six or more days a week - before sitting meditation gets serious airtime. That ordering is deliberate and smart:

  • Bodily sensation is an easy target. Pressure of the chair, warmth of a hand - these are big, findable signals. The breath at the nostrils is a whisper by comparison. You train the noticing muscle on the loud stuff first.
  • It is the same rep in disguise. Attention rests on a zone, wanders to lunch, you notice, you return to the zone. Identical mechanics to Session 1's loop - just with a target you cannot lose.
  • It builds the tension map. Most people discover in week one that their shoulders or jaw have been clenched for years. That map pays rent for the rest of the course.

"Nothing to feel" is a finding, not a failure. Some zones report silence - a numb patch, a blank forearm. Fine. "No sensation detectable" is data you noticed, which means the attention showed up and did its job. Note it and move on.

Self-studyBody scan vs progressive muscle relaxation2 min read▶

People often confuse the body scan with progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), the older clinical technique. They tour the same body but do opposite things:

Body scanPMR
MoveObserve each zone as it isDeliberately tense each muscle group, then release
GoalTrain noticing; relaxation optionalProduce relaxation directly
If tension staysThat is a valid findingThat is the thing to fix

Fun honest aside: PMR was one of the active comparators in Goyal 2014 - and meditation showed no advantage over it on any outcome. The humble tense-and-release drill from the 1930s held its own against the practice with all the headlines. If PMR is what relaxes you, do PMR; the body scan earns its place by training attention, not by out-relaxing anything.

Part 3 · covers how long to sit

The duration ladder 4 min live

How long should a beginner sit? Long enough to get reps, short enough that you actually show up tomorrow. Your plan's weeks 1-2 use a three-rung ladder: 3 minutes → 5 minutes → 8 minutes. Climb a rung only when the current one feels routine - as in, you no longer negotiate with yourself about starting. There is no schedule for climbing; some people sit at 5 minutes for a month and that is a working practice.

LiveThe ladder, calibrated against the clinical dose3 min▶

For calibration, MBSR's own sitting-meditation ladder ran 10-15 minutes in week 2 → 15-20 in week 3 → 20 in week 4 - on top of daily 45-minute body scans. That is the dose most famous studies measured, and it is a part-time job. Your ladder starts far below it on purpose: the goal of a starter plan is a surviving habit, not a replicated study protocol.

  • 3 min - the entry rung. Too small to dread, big enough for a dozen reps.
  • 5 min - the first rung that feels like a real sit. Most people live here in week 1.
  • 8 min - enough room for the mind to settle, wander, and get caught repeatedly. A solid weeks-1-2 ceiling.

Plan for missed days now. Across 43 real studies, participants completed about 64% of assigned practice (Parsons 2017) - and those were people in structured programs with instructors. Missing roughly a third of days is the documented human norm, not a personal failing. Build the plan assuming misses: a missed day costs one day, drama about the missed day costs the week.

Real world

The 64% mindset in practice: a learner who sits 5 of 7 days at 5 minutes is outperforming the published adherence average. Say that sentence to yourself before declaring the week a failure.

Practice 1 of 2

Your first body scan ✦ 12 min · everyone does this

Ten minutes, seven zones, no soundtrack needed. Lying down is the classic position for this one; a chair works fine if lying down means napping.

Set a 10-minute timer. Phone on do-not-disturb. Lie on your back or sit in your chair, eyes closed or half-closed.

Start at the feet. Rest attention there and just report to yourself what is present: pressure, warmth, tingling, sock seams, nothing. Do not wiggle to generate sensation - listen for what is already there.

Move up roughly one zone per minute: feet → legs → hips → belly and chest → hands and arms → shoulders and neck → face and head. Imprecise timing is fine; the route matters more than the stopwatch.

When the mind wanders (it will, constantly), notice, and return to the zone you left off - not back to the feet. The catch is the rep, exactly as in Session 1.

At the timer, take one breath of the whole body at once, then note two things: which zone had the most sensation, and which had the least. That is your first tension map entry.

Real world

Typical first-scan report: fell into thought for what felt like three full zones, jaw turned out to be clenched, one forearm reported absolutely nothing. All three are textbook findings. The scan worked.

Lock in posture and dose ✦ 6 min · builds your 4-week plan

Step 1 gave your plan a why, step 2 gave it an anchor and a time slot. Step 3 gives it a body: the posture you will actually use and the duration rung you will actually start on. Written down, these stop being daily decisions - and un-decided mornings are where practices die.

Pick your posture from Part 1 - the leftmost option on the diagram your body is genuinely happy in. For most people that is the chair, and the chair is a complete answer.

Pick your starting rung: 3, 5, or 8 minutes. When torn between two, take the smaller - climbing up feels like progress, climbing down feels like defeat, so start where up is the likely direction.

Schedule 2-3 body scans this week alongside the daily sits - they can replace a sit on tired days. Then fill in the template below and add it to your plan card.

Homework

Before Session 4 ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Daily sits at your chosen duration and posture - the step 3 numbers, no re-deciding each morning. Keep counting the catches.
  • 2-3 body scans this week, 10 minutes each, using the route from Practice 1. Lying down is allowed and traditional.
  • Track one thing across the scans: where does tension hide in your body? Jaw, shoulders, belly, hands - write down your top spot and bring the answer to Session 4.
  • Optional: if your current posture nags at you, test-drive one alternative from Part 1 for a single sit and compare honestly.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The point of the body scan is to...

The scan trains noticing, not relaxing - relaxation is an optional side effect. Tense shoulders and numb forearms are valid findings, not errors. (Tense-and-release on command is PMR, a different technique.)

2 · Ten minutes into a sit, your knee starts hurting - sharp and getting worse. You should...

Pain gets an immediate adjustment - no heroism. It is fidget-itch (the restless urge to move) that you sit through, because it fades under observation and noticing it is a rep. Pain gets louder; believe it.

3 · The first formal practice MBSR assigns its students is...

The body scan comes first - bodily sensation is a big, findable target, so the noticing muscle gets trained on easy reps before the subtle breath takes over. Sitting builds up from week 2 onward.
